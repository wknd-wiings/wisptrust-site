Do I really need one?

If your firm handles customer financial information — and a tax practice does by definition — the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314) requires a written information security program with a designated Qualified Individual. On the IRS side, Form W-12 line 11 asks every paid preparer to attest to having a written plan when applying for or renewing a PTIN, and all PTINs expire December 31. IRS Publications 4557 and 5708 explain the expectation in the IRS's own words. So for a practicing firm, yes — this is a standing duty, not an optional extra.

I'm a one-person firm — does this apply to me?

A smaller version of it does, and we'll tell you exactly which parts. Under 16 CFR 314.6, firms maintaining information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from a few written-program elements (the written incident response plan requirement and certain testing provisions, among them). The core duties are not exempted at any size: having a program, designating a Qualified Individual, running safeguards, overseeing vendors — and the FTC's breach-notification duty in 314.4(j) applies to every firm. The IRS attestation has no small-firm exemption. Your plan states your 314.6 position in writing, so what applies to you is documented, not assumed.

Why not a $39 template?

A template is a document written for a hypothetical firm; the work of matching it to your actual software, devices, vendors, and practices — the risk assessment, the inventories, the verification — is still entirely yours, and that is most of the job. What we sell is the finished work product: built from your answers, with your unverified items listed honestly as gaps instead of written up as facts, and every fact read back with you on the call, so you sign something true. If you will genuinely finish a template yourself, it's a fair option; if it would sit unfinished, it isn't a plan.

I already have an IT person — isn't this theirs?

Your IT person runs your technology; this is the documentation layer they typically don't produce: the written risk assessment, the vendor register with contract clauses checked, the Qualified Individual's annual report, the incident plan with its reporting clocks. The Safeguards Rule requires a written program under a named QI — a document, not a device setting. Your IT person actually makes our verification step easier: several answers are confirmed with their help. We document what they do; we don't replace them.

What happens if I get breached anyway?

First, honesty: no plan prevents every breach, and anyone who guarantees that is misrepresenting the product. What the plan gives you is the response, ready before you need it: a contact card (attorney, insurer, bank, IRS liaison), a quick card for the first hour, and the reporting clocks spelled out — the IRS stakeholder liaison by the next business day, the FTC within 30 days if the event involves 500 or more consumers' unencrypted information, your states' notifications per the matrix built for your client states. After an incident, firms are judged on what they can show: their program, their evidence, and their response. The plan is exactly that record.
