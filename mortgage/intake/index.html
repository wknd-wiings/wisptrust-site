Do not send us any borrower data. Ever.

That means: no borrower names, no Social Security numbers, no loan files or anything from one, no credit reports, no pay stubs or bank statements, no passwords or login codes, and not your NMLS ID number itself (we will only ever ask whether it is recorded in your own records).

Why we are strict about this: your borrowers trusted you with their information for their loan — never for this service. Sharing it with us is never necessary to build your plan, and keeping it out of both our systems protects your borrowers, you, and us.

Do I really need one?

If your firm handles customer financial information — and a mortgage brokerage does by definition — the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314) requires a written information security program with a designated Qualified Individual. Mortgage brokers and lenders are named financial institutions under the rule. State licensing examinations test for exactly this program, and loan purchasers increasingly ask for it by contract. So for a practicing firm, yes — this is a standing duty, not an optional extra.

I'm a one-person firm — does this apply to me?

A smaller version of it does, and we'll tell you exactly which parts. Under 16 CFR 314.6, firms maintaining information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from a few written-program elements (the written incident response plan requirement and certain testing provisions, among them). The core duties are not exempted at any size: having a program, designating a Qualified Individual, running safeguards, overseeing vendors — and the FTC's breach-notification duty in 314.4(j) applies to every firm. Your plan states your 314.6 position in writing, so what applies to you is documented, not assumed.

The questionnaire — about 30 minutes, answered firsthand by whoever knows each part of your firm — about how your firm operates: your people and roles, your software, your devices, your vendors, your office setup. Plus an email address we can reach you at. For a handful of settings (multi-factor authentication, encryption, backups), answer from the settings screen itself where you can, so the plan states facts, not recollections; if we need anything more, we'll ask by email. That's the whole input — there is no call.

Building your plan never requires a single borrower's information.

Why not a $39 template?

A template is a document written for a hypothetical firm; the work of matching it to your actual software, devices, vendors, and practices — the risk assessment, the inventories, the verification — is still entirely yours, and that is most of the job. What we sell is the finished work product: built from your answers, with your unverified items listed honestly as gaps instead of written up as facts, and every answer checked by a Client Verification Specialist before the plan is built, so you sign something true. If you will genuinely finish a template yourself, it's a fair option; if it would sit unfinished, it isn't a plan.

I already have an IT person — isn't this theirs?

Your IT person runs your technology; this is the documentation layer they typically don't produce: the written risk assessment, the vendor register with contract clauses checked, the Qualified Individual's annual report, the incident plan with its reporting clocks. The Safeguards Rule requires a written program under a named QI — a document, not a device setting. Your IT person actually makes our verification step easier: several answers describe the settings they manage. We document what they do; we don't replace them.

What happens if I get breached anyway?

First, honesty: no plan prevents every breach, and anyone who guarantees that is misrepresenting the product. What the plan gives you is the response, ready before you need it: a contact card (attorney, insurer, bank, state licensing regulator), a quick card for the first hour, and the reporting clocks spelled out — your state licensing regulator promptly, per your license conditions; the FTC within 30 days if the event involves 500 or more consumers' unencrypted information; your states' notifications per the matrix built for your borrower states; and your loan purchasers or warehouse partners wherever your contracts require notice. After an incident, firms are judged on what they can show: their program, their evidence, and their response. The plan is exactly that record.

Who is my Qualified Individual?

You are — we recommend the owner hold the designation, and the plan is built to support the owner in it: the designation document, the annual QI report to sign off, the training and review rhythm. We never serve as a firm's Qualified Individual; that accountability has to live inside the firm, and our job is to make it manageable.

What happens at the annual review?

Your plan is reviewed at least annually, on the anniversary of its adoption. If you're on the keeping-current plan: we re-run the annual review, update the plan for whatever changed — people, software, rules — and refresh the QI annual report for your sign-off, so what you hand a licensing examiner or a loan purchaser is current. If you're not, the plan is yours to maintain, and the annual review is available whenever you want it.

What if the rules change?

We watch the sources monthly — the Safeguards Rule on the eCFR, FTC and Federal Register announcements, CFPB guidance for mortgage markets, and the state laws in your plan's notification matrix — with a deep re-verification every January. If a change affects your plan, it reaches you as a dated, versioned plan amendment under the Safeguards Year, explained in plain English, for your signature the same way as the original adoption; your signed plan is never silently edited, and your packet keeps every version. Your annual review then re-issues the full plan incorporating everything — so no client is ever more than a year behind the current rule, and the amendments cover the space between.
