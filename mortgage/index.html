Why a mortgage brokerage needs one

  • The Safeguards Rule names mortgage brokers as financial institutions — a written program is a standing duty, at every firm size.
  • State licensing examinations test for it. Loan purchasers and warehouse partners increasingly require it by contract.
  • Under 5,000 consumer records? Four written elements ease under 16 CFR 314.6 — your plan states your position in writing, and keeps the elements documented anyway.
  • Your owner is the Qualified Individual. We build the paperwork that supports them — we never serve as your QI, and nothing here is "exam-proof" or guaranteed. It is your program, built true.

Running without one is like driving without insurance until you have a wreck. Nothing reminds you it's missing — until the day something happens. This program has been required since June 2023, and there is no annual deadline to hide behind: the exposure just sits there, every day. Enforcement usually arrives after a breach — and a breach touching 500 or more consumers has to be reported to the FTC within 30 days. That is when the question gets asked: where is your written program? Waiting for the wreck isn't a plan. Having the plan is.

Questions brokers ask us

Do you need my NMLS number or any borrower files?

No — neither, ever. We ask whether your NMLS ID is recorded in your own records; we never collect the number, a loan file, a credit report, or a single borrower's name. Our intake is built to refuse that data.

Who is my Qualified Individual?

You are — we recommend the owner hold the designation. The plan is built to support the owner in it. We never serve as a firm's Qualified Individual.

Will this satisfy my state examiner?

Examiners test whether a real written program exists and matches how you operate. Your plan is built from your verified answers against the rule text itself, with a Basis & Sources page your attorney can check line by line. No honest builder can promise an examination outcome — and we won't.

Do banks follow the same rule?

Banks follow a sister rulebook. The same federal law — the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act — requires every financial institution to maintain a written information security program. Banks answer to the Interagency Guidelines enforced by their own regulators (the OCC, Federal Reserve, or FDIC; credit unions follow the NCUA's version), so they're exempt from the FTC's rule. Your firm isn't a bank, so the FTC Safeguards Rule is your version of that same requirement — and it's the one WispTrust builds your plan to.

I've operated this long without one — why now?

Because running without a written program is like driving without insurance until you have a wreck: nothing reminds you it's missing until the day something happens. The requirement has stood since June 2023, and unlike a tax deadline, there is no date on the calendar that warns you first — an exam can land in any cycle, and after a serious breach the reporting clock runs in days. Waiting for the wreck isn't a plan. Getting the program written, from how your firm actually operates, is.
